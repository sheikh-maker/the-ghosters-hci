The Ghosters · Human–Computer Interaction

We haunt the bugs,

Hello, and welcome to our HCI project journal. We are five UTM students exploring how thoughtful interface design can make everyday technology clearer, more inclusive, and easier to use.

Why “The Ghosters”?

A good interface often fades into the background: people can focus on their task instead of fighting the screen. We chose the name “The Ghosters” to represent that quiet work. We look for the small friction points hiding in an interface, then try to make them disappear.

Our motto is “We haunt the bugs, not the users.” This public blog brings together our group’s first post and our Project 1 proposal.

Project 1 · Proposal

Checkout should
work for everyone.

01

Self-checkout kiosks are meant to make shopping faster, but many shoppers still get stuck, confused or frustrated at the very last step of their visit. For this project, our group focuses on the self-checkout kiosk at AEON supermarkets in Malaysia. Under the theme of Cognitive/Emotion-Adaptive Interaction, we propose AEON GraceMarketMate, a kiosk that notices when a shopper is struggling and adapts its guidance and help in real time. This post sets out the problems we found, our proposed solution, and the users who would benefit most.

01 / Problem space

A small screen can
make a big shop harder.

Self-checkout asks shoppers to identify products, scan or search, resolve mistakes, and confirm a basket—often while keeping track of what they have already done. Clear steps, visible recovery, and accessible controls can make that process easier to follow.

Three usability problems

Where the flow
loses people.

The examples below focus on how kiosk screens, basket controls, and physical reach can affect a shopping task.

01▤

Finding an item

Crowded item lookup
and interruptions

Product categories, produce look-up, promotions, bag prompts, and scan guidance can compete for attention. When secondary choices interrupt the main task, shoppers must work harder to find and add the right item. Working-memory limits help explain why competing decisions can add effort (Sweller, 1988). See kiosk photo 1 for an example of a checkout screen.

Design principlesVisibility · Constraints · Progressive disclosure
Usability goalsEfficiency · Learnability · Memorability
UX impact Too many choices at once can feel tiring, confusing, or pressuring.
02↶

Correcting a mistake

Hard-to-fix basket
and scan errors

A shopper may scan an item twice, choose the wrong produce entry, or need to change a quantity. If correction tools are hard to find, the person may have to repeat steps or ask staff for help. Easy undo and direct recovery support user control (Nielsen, 1994). See kiosk photo 2 for the checkout-station context.

Design principlesUser control · Feedback · Error recovery
Usability goalsSafety · Efficiency · User control
UX impact Unclear recovery can create worry about price, embarrassment, or loss of control.
03◉

Reaching and confirming

Small controls and
unclear feedback

Small touch targets, low contrast, unclear response after a tap, or controls placed beyond comfortable reach can make a kiosk difficult to use. Where a display is fixed high or controls sit near the top edge, wheelchair users or shorter shoppers may need to stretch or ask for help. W3C guidance explains how target size and spacing can reduce accidental activation (W3C, 2023). See kiosk photo 3 for a shopper interacting with a screen.

Design principlesVisibility · Feedback · Accessible interaction
Usability goalsEffectiveness · Safety · Inclusiveness
UX impact People may feel excluded when the kiosk does not respond clearly or comfortably.

02 / User experience

It is more than
getting through a screen.

At a busy checkout, people want to finish confidently and keep the line moving. Extra decisions can add mental effort; unclear recovery can make a simple slip feel costly; and a screen that is hard to reach or read can take away a shopper's independence.

These effects matter for wheelchair users, older adults and people with low vision, and shoppers who do not use self-checkout often.

03 / Proposed solution

Meet Grace MarketMate.

A supermarket self-checkout concept that keeps the next step clear, makes basket edits forgiving, and lets shoppers listen to spoken guidance if they want it.

Problem 01

Too much at once

→ Focus item search. Show essential information first and keep promotional prompts optional.

Problem 02

Hard to recover

→ Edit quantities, remove an item, and undo the last change directly in the basket.

Problem 03

Hard to reach or read

→ Offer a reach-friendly layout that groups key actions in a full-width lower panel, with larger controls, strong contrast, clear tap confirmation, and optional voice narration. Pair it with an adjustable, tilting display mount where the kiosk hardware allows it.

04 / Target users

Designed around
real differences.

These groups are specific shoppers who may use a self-checkout differently. We will validate the needs through observation and feedback.

01
♿

Wheelchair users
and people with limited reach

Reach layout groups key basket actions in a full-width lower panel with larger controls. Stores should pair the interface with an adjustable, tilting display where possible, then validate reach through observation with wheelchair users.

Reach · independence · feedback
02
◉

Older adults and

Larger type, strong contrast, distinct touch controls, and optional spoken prompts can make product details and checkout steps easier to perceive.

05 / Sustainability connection

Inclusion is part of
good design.

Our contribution is a design proposal, not a measured sustainability outcome. The links below explain why the project is relevant.

SDG 10

Reduced inequalities

UN Target 10.2 calls for promoting social, economic, and political inclusion regardless of age or disability. MarketMate explores how a self-checkout can offer more people a clear, independent path.

Read Goal 10 ↗
SDG 12

Responsible consumption

UN Target 12.3 includes reducing food waste at retail and consumer levels. A clear basket review could help shoppers catch accidental duplicate or unwanted items; whether that changes waste needs to be tested.

Read Goal 12 ↗
UTM · ESG

Social and governance

The concept relates to UTM's ESG framing through accessible interaction (Social) and visible item, quantity, and price changes (Governance). Environmental impact is indirect and unverified.
