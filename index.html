The Ghosters · Human–Computer Interaction

We haunt the bugs,
not the users.

Hello, and welcome to our HCI project journal. We are five UTM students exploring how thoughtful interface design can make everyday technology clearer, more inclusive, and easier to use.

Why “The Ghosters”?

A good interface often fades into the background: people can focus on their task instead of fighting the screen. We chose the name “The Ghosters” to represent that quiet work. We look for the small friction points hiding in an interface, then try to make them disappear.

Our motto is “We haunt the bugs, not the users.” This public blog brings together our group’s first post and our Project 1 proposal.

Project 1 · Proposal

Checkout should
work for everyone.

01

Self-checkout kiosks are meant to make shopping faster, but many shoppers still get stuck, confused or frustrated at the very last step of their visit. For this project, our group focuses on self-checkout at AEON supermarkets in Malaysia. Under the theme of Cognitive/Emotion-Adaptive Interaction, we propose AEON GraceMarketMate: a smart-trolley concept paired with the self-checkout kiosk. It keeps item information visible and offers clear, adaptive guidance through shopping and payment. This post sets out the problems we found, our proposed solution, and the users who would benefit most.

See what gets in the way ↓

01 / Problem space

A small screen can
make a big shop harder.

AEON supermarkets in Malaysia are pushing digital checkout. AEON has said it wants deep integration between its app and its stores, to the point of enabling self-checkout. AEON members can also show their points barcode at the cashier or self-service scanner to earn 1 AEON Point per RM1 spent. An AEON shopper may therefore handle several steps in one visit: scanning items, bagging, points or membership, and choosing a payment method. Our project focus is the AEON supermarket self-checkout kiosk. Self-checkout is meant to cut waiting time, but a kiosk that is slow, unclear, or unforgiving can bring the queue and the stress right back. We treat the four problems below as initial observations to investigate, not as claims about every store.

Four usability problems

Where the checkout
loses people.

Each problem below is about the AEON self-checkout kiosk. For each one we show the aspect affected, the design principles violated, the usability goals affected, and how our proposed system responds.

01

Aspect · Cognitive and emotional

Long queues during checkout
because items are scanned one by one

At a conventional self-checkout, shoppers scan each product separately, then check the screen and bag their items. Scanning and correcting errors one item at a time can slow each transaction, extend the queue, and make shoppers feel rushed.

Design principles violated
  • Efficiency: repeated manual scanning adds time to checkout.
  • Feedback: unclear scan confirmation can make shoppers repeat an action.
  • Simplicity: a long sequence of individual scans adds avoidable steps.
Usability goals affected
  • Efficiency: shoppers may wait longer to reach an available kiosk.
  • Satisfaction: long queues can create frustration and pressure.
  • Effectiveness: rushing can lead to missed or duplicate scans.
Affected usersShoppers during busy periods, including people who prefer self-service but want a quicker checkout.
How the Smart Trolley helps Items are detected as they are placed in the trolley. Shoppers review the running list and scan one checkout barcode, reducing item-by-item scanning at the kiosk.
02

Aspect · Cognitive and emotional

Difficulty understanding
self-checkout instructions

Shoppers may be unsure what to do next when instructions, item status, and payment choices are difficult to interpret. Unfamiliar wording or limited language options can lead to misunderstood steps, hesitation, or requests for help.

Design principles violated
  • Simplicity: unclear or dense instructions make the next action hard to identify.
  • Visibility and feedback: the screen should clearly show what happened and what to do next.
  • Recognition rather than recall: shoppers should not need to remember instructions from earlier screens.
Usability goals affected
  • Learnability: first-time users may need extra time to understand the process.
  • Effectiveness: misunderstood steps can interrupt or prevent checkout.
  • Satisfaction: uncertainty can make self-service feel stressful.
Affected usersShoppers using a language they are less comfortable with, first-time users, and people who prefer clear step-by-step guidance.
How the Smart Trolley helps The trolley offers simple step-by-step instructions, clear confirmation after item detection, language choices, and larger text and buttons.
03

Aspect · Emotional and financial

Fear of being
overcharged

When the item list and prices are difficult to check, shoppers may worry that an item was counted twice, has the wrong price, or pushed the total beyond their budget. Seeing the total only at the end can make it harder to spot a problem early.

Design principles violated
  • Visibility: individual prices and the running total should stay easy to see.
  • Feedback: the system should confirm each detected item and price change.
  • User control: shoppers need a chance to review the bill before paying.
Usability goals affected
  • Effectiveness: shoppers need to verify the bill matches the items they chose.
  • Satisfaction: a clear, reviewable total supports confidence in payment.
  • Utility: a live total helps shoppers manage their budget while shopping.
Affected usersBudget-conscious shoppers and anyone who wants to verify their purchases before paying.
How the Smart Trolley helps The screen shows every detected item, its price, and the updated total. Before checkout, shoppers can review the complete list and confirm the amount.
04

Aspect · Cognitive and emotional

Difficulty removing an
unwanted or duplicate item

If a product is added by mistake or appears twice, shoppers may not find a clear way to remove it themselves. Requiring staff approval interrupts the transaction and can feel stressful while others wait.

Design principles violated
  • User control: shoppers should be able to correct an item entry themselves.
  • Error recovery: the system should make accidental entries easy to undo.
  • Feedback: the updated list and total should be shown after a removal.
Usability goals affected
  • Effectiveness: shoppers need to correct the list so it matches their basket.
  • Efficiency: waiting for staff for a routine edit adds time.
  • Satisfaction: easy recovery reduces frustration and embarrassment.
Affected usersShoppers who make an accidental selection or need to correct an item before checkout.
How the Smart Trolley helps Shoppers can edit or remove an item directly from the trolley list before confirming checkout. The total updates immediately; staff help remains available when an item needs verification.

02 / User experience

How it feels
to use.

Checkout is the last step of a shopping trip, so a shopper arrives already carrying a basket, a budget and a queue behind them. When the kiosk goes wrong, the cost is emotional as well as practical.

Usability problem 1

Queuing at the self-checkout

What we see. People line up at the self-checkout stations and wait for a free kiosk.

What the shopper feels. Impatient and stressed, especially at peak hours. Seeing the line behind them can make a shopper rush, and rushing leads to mistakes.

Reflects: long queue during checkout because of scanning items.

Usability problem 1Usability problem 3

Scanning one by one, and checking the bill

What we see. Shoppers scan their items one by one, so each kiosk takes a long time and the queue keeps growing.

What the shopper feels. Because every item is scanned by hand, shoppers keep double-checking the screen to make sure nothing was scanned twice and that they have not overspent. This adds worry on top of the waiting.

Reflects: long queue (1) and fear of being overcharged (3).

Usability problem 4

Needing staff to delete an item

What we see. A shopper uses the touchscreen. If an item is scanned by mistake, the shopper cannot delete it alone and must call staff for help.

What the shopper feels. Frustrated and a little embarrassed, because they have to wait for assistance while other shoppers are waiting behind them.

Reflects: difficulty removing an unwanted or duplicate item (problem 4).

Usability problem 2

Unclear payment screen

What we see. Several payment choices appear together, and the screen does not clearly show which items the shopper has already scanned.

What the shopper feels. A bit confused. They are unsure which items they have scanned and which they have not scanned.

Reflects: difficulty understanding self-checkout instructions (problem 2).

Usability problem 2

Language barrier at the start

What we see. The welcome screen offers only Bahasa Malaysia and English before the shopper begins.

What the shopper feels. Lost and unsure. A shopper who is not comfortable in these languages may not understand the checkout steps that follow.

Reflects: difficulty understanding self-checkout instructions (problem 2).

03 / Proposed solution

Meet the Smart Trolley.

The Smart Trolley is a shopping trolley with built-in item detection and a simple screen. Shoppers put items straight into the trolley, and it detects each one, shows its price, and keeps a running total. At the end, shoppers review their list, confirm it, and pay at an AEON self-checkout kiosk by scanning a barcode generated by the trolley. It removes the item-by-item scanning, keeps the bill visible, and is designed to be easy for everyone to understand.

Smart Trolley Steps

1 Put items in→2 Review the list→3 Confirm checkout→4 Scan barcode and pay
01

Smart Trolley feature

Automatic item
detection and price

Customers put items directly into the Smart Trolley. The trolley detects each item automatically and displays its price on the screen, so nobody has to scan products one by one.

SolvesUsability problem 1
Benefit Less manual work, less waiting, and less mental effort to keep track of what has been scanned.
02

Smart Trolley feature

Automatic
running total

The system calculates the total price of everything in the trolley and updates it every time an item is added. Customers can see how much they have spent at any moment while they shop.

SolvesUsability problem 3
Benefit Shoppers can stay within their budget and feel confident about their final bill.
03

Smart Trolley feature

Review, edit and confirm
before checkout

When customers press the checkout button, the system generates a complete list of purchased items, shows the total price, and asks for confirmation before going further. Customers can edit or remove unwanted or duplicate items from the list; the total updates before they confirm.

SolvesUsability problem 3 · Usability problem 4
Benefit Customers stay in control and know exactly what they are paying for.
04

Smart Trolley feature

Pay at the kiosk with
a trolley barcode

Customers pay at a self-checkout kiosk by scanning a barcode generated by the trolley. The shopper does not scan each product again at the kiosk, so the kiosk stays free for the next customer sooner.

SolvesUsability problem 1
Benefit A shorter time at the kiosk and a shorter queue behind it.
05

Smart Trolley feature

Translation options
in different languages

The system offers translation options so shoppers can read the screen in the language they are most comfortable with, instead of being limited to the languages on the current kiosk.

SolvesUsability problem 2
Benefit Shoppers with language barriers can understand each step and avoid wrong actions.
06

Smart Trolley feature

Friendly interface for
elderly customers

The interface uses larger text, bigger buttons, and simple instructions, so elderly customers and first-time users can follow each step without feeling lost.

SolvesUsability problem 2
Benefit Older and less experienced shoppers can use the system confidently and independently.

04 / Target users

Designed around
real differences.

These are the shoppers who use the AEON self-checkout in different ways, and who we expect to benefit most from the Smart Trolley. We will check these needs through observation and feedback.

01

Target user

Supermarket customers who
prefer self-service shopping

These shoppers like to shop and check out on their own, without waiting for a cashier. They choose self-service to save time, so a slow kiosk, a long queue, or needing staff for a small mistake works against what they want.

Related problemsUsability problem 1 · Usability problem 4
How the Smart Trolley helps Items are detected automatically and payment is done by scanning one barcode, so self-service really is faster.
02

Target user

Customers from different
language backgrounds

Shoppers in Malaysia come from many language backgrounds. If the kiosk screen is not in a language they are comfortable with, they may not understand the instructions and may press the wrong option or ask for help.

Related problemsUsability problem 2
How the Smart Trolley helps Translation options let shoppers read each step in the language they prefer.
03

Target user

Budget-conscious shoppers who
want to monitor their spending

These shoppers want to know how much they are spending while they shop. When prices and totals are only clear at the end, they worry about going over budget, or about being charged twice for the same item.

Related problemsUsability problem 3
How the Smart Trolley helps Each item's price and a running total are always on the screen, and the full list is shown for confirmation before checkout.
04

Target user

Elderly customers who need a
simple, easy-to-use interface

Elderly customers may find small text, small buttons, and many steps on a screen hard to use. If the kiosk feels like it was not made for them, they may avoid it or depend on staff.

Related problemsUsability problem 2
How the Smart Trolley helps Larger text, bigger buttons, and simple instructions make each step easier to see and follow.
