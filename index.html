The Ghosters · Human–Computer Interaction

We haunt the bugs,

Hello, and welcome to our HCI project journal. We are five UTM students exploring how thoughtful interface design can make everyday technology clearer, more inclusive, and easier to use.

Why “The Ghosters”?

A good interface often fades into the background: people can focus on their task instead of fighting the screen. We chose the name “The Ghosters” to represent that quiet work. We look for the small friction points hiding in an interface, then try to make them disappear.

Our motto is “We haunt the bugs, not the users.” This public blog brings together our group’s first post and our Project 1 proposal.

Project 1 · Proposal

Checkout should
work for everyone.

01

Self-checkout kiosks are meant to make shopping faster, but many shoppers still get stuck, confused or frustrated at the very last step of their visit. For this project, our group focuses on the self-checkout kiosk at AEON supermarkets in Malaysia. Under the theme of Cognitive/Emotion-Adaptive Interaction, we propose AEON GraceMarketMate, a kiosk that notices when a shopper is struggling and adapts its guidance and help in real time. This post sets out the problems we found, our proposed solution, and the users who would benefit most.

See what gets in the way ↓

01 / Problem space

A small screen can
make a big shop harder.

AEON supermarkets in Malaysia are pushing digital checkout. AEON has said it wants deep integration between its app and its stores, to the point of enabling self-checkout. AEON members can also show their points barcode at the cashier or self-service scanner to earn 1 AEON Point per RM1 spent. An AEON shopper may therefore handle several steps in one visit: scanning items, bagging, points or membership, and choosing a payment method. Our project focus is the AEON supermarket self-checkout kiosk. Self-checkout is meant to cut waiting time, but a kiosk that is slow, unclear, or unforgiving can bring the queue and the stress right back. We treat the four problems below as initial observations to investigate, not as claims about every store.

Four usability problems

Where the checkout
loses people.

Each problem below is about the AEON self-checkout kiosk. For each one we show the aspect affected, the design principles violated, the usability goals affected, and how our proposed system responds.

01

Aspect · Emotional and cognitive

Long queue during checkout
because of scanning items

Customers may feel frustrated, stressed, or impatient in a long queue, especially during peak hours. Waiting gets longer when customers scan items one by one or run into scanning errors. They must also scan each product, check the displayed information, and make sure every item was registered, which needs continuous attention and causes high cognitive workload.

Design principles violated
  • Efficiency: the checkout takes longer than necessary.
  • User control: customers have limited control over the waiting time.
  • Feedback: customers may not know how long checkout will take.
  • Recognition rather than recall: customers must remember which products are already scanned.
  • Simplicity: the process has multiple manual steps that could be simplified.
Usability goals affected
  • Efficiency: customers cannot finish their shopping quickly.
  • Satisfaction: long waits lead to frustration and a negative experience.
  • Cognitive workload: constant checking keeps mental effort high.
How RFID solves it Products are detected automatically when placed in the trolley, so there is less manual interaction and less mental workload.
02

Aspect · Cognitive and emotional

Difficulty removing
accidentally scanned items

Customers may scan or add the same item twice. To remove the extra item, the self-checkout kiosk may need staff assistance or approval. This interrupts checkout and makes customers feel frustrated, especially when other customers are waiting in the queue.

Design principles violated
  • User control: customers cannot correct their own mistakes.
  • Error recovery: there is no easy way to fix an accidental item entry.
  • Feedback: customers may not get clear instructions on how to remove the unwanted item.
Usability goals affected
  • Efficiency: waiting for staff increases checkout time.
  • Effectiveness: customers may struggle to correct their shopping list accurately.
  • Satisfaction: waiting for help causes frustration and dissatisfaction.
How the new system solves it The trolley shows a real-time list of detected items. Customers can review it and remove accidental or duplicate items on the trolley screen, and the total price updates automatically. For security, unusual removals can be flagged for staff verification.
03

Aspect · Cognitive

Difficulty understanding the
self-checkout instructions

Some customers struggle to understand the instructions on the self-checkout screen, particularly if they are unfamiliar with the system or have language barriers.

Design principles violated
  • Simplicity: instructions may use complicated wording or show too much information, so customers are unsure what to do next.
  • Recognition rather than recall: customers may have to remember instructions from earlier screens while doing later steps, which is hard for first-time users.
Usability goals affected
  • Learnability: customers unfamiliar with self-checkout or facing language barriers take longer to understand the process.
  • Effectiveness: if instructions are misunderstood, customers may do the wrong action, skip a step, or fail to complete checkout.
Affected usersShoppers with language barriers or limited experience with technology.
Proposed solution Simple instructions, clear icons, and multilingual options on the trolley screen.
04

Aspect · Emotional

Fear of
being overcharged

Customers may worry that an item was scanned twice or that the displayed price is wrong. This reduces their confidence in the self-checkout system.

Design principles violated
  • Visibility: customers cannot clearly see whether an item was scanned once or twice, or whether the price is correct, so they are unsure about their final bill.
  • Feedback: customers may not get clear confirmation that an item was scanned or added, or that the price updated correctly. The system should respond clearly and immediately after an action or change.
Usability goals affected
  • Effectiveness: if customers cannot verify items and prices, they may pay for duplicates or wrong amounts.
  • Satisfaction: customers feel worried or dissatisfied when unsure they are charged correctly. The experience should make them feel confident.
Affected usersBusy shoppers and shoppers unfamiliar with self-service technology.
Proposed solution The Smart Shopping Trolley displays each detected item, its individual price, and the running total, so customers can review their purchases before confirming checkout.

02 / User experience

How it feels
to use.

Checkout is the last step of a shopping trip, so a shopper arrives already carrying a basket, a budget and a queue behind them. When the kiosk goes wrong, the cost is emotional as well as practical.

Step What the shopper feels UX goals not met What GraceMarketMate does
Find or scan items Annoyed and stuck when a bagging error stops them with no clear next step. Satisfying, helpful Shows a friendly message and a short animation, with one retry button.
Review the basket Confused and unsure what they have already done, which adds mental effort. Supportive, motivating A progress bar and basket summary show where they are.
Confirm and pay Pressured, embarrassed, or treated like a suspect when the machine flags them. Older shoppers may find the kiosk intimidating. Emotionally fulfilling, supportive Offers help quietly, saves progress, and uses supportive wording.

Who feels
this most.

  • Wheelchair users may struggle with a screen that is hard to reach, which can take away their independence.
  • Older adults may feel the kiosk is not made for them [8].
  • Low-vision shoppers may find small text and low contrast hard to read.
  • Infrequent users do not know the flow, so each extra decision costs more effort.

Our UX goal

Confident, supported
and in control.

GraceMarketMate should make shoppers feel confident, supported and in control, and it should keep the line moving. A shopper who makes a simple mistake should be able to recover calmly instead of feeling judged.

03 / Proposed solution

Meet Grace MarketMate.

A supermarket self-checkout concept that keeps the next step clear, makes basket edits forgiving, and lets shoppers listen to spoken guidance if they want it.

Problem 01

Too much at once

→ Focus item search. Show essential information first and keep promotional prompts optional.

Problem 02

Hard to recover

→ Edit quantities, remove an item, and undo the last change directly in the basket.

Problem 03

Hard to reach or read

→ Offer a reach-friendly layout that groups key actions in a full-width lower panel, with larger controls, strong contrast, clear tap confirmation, and optional voice narration. Pair it with an adjustable, tilting display mount where the kiosk hardware allows it.
